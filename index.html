Medical cost segmentation · intervention targeting

The most expensive segment in the book is also the smallest

Smoking and obesity do not add together — they multiply. The 145 members who carry both risk factors are 11% of this book and 34% of its spend. This analysis sizes that segment, tests whether the effect is real, and ranks five candidate interventions against it.

Read this first. The source file is the widely circulated Medical Cost Personal Datasets teaching set, which is simulated rather than drawn from real claims. Findings below are real properties of the data and the methods are the ones I would use on a live book, but no conclusion here describes an actual insurer. One finding — the BMI 30 discontinuity — is almost certainly an artifact of how the file was generated, and is reported as such rather than quietly dropped.

What the data says

Six findings

Smoking and obesity multiply rather than add

Being obese costs a non-smoker $879 a year. It costs a smoker $20,195 — a 23-fold difference in the same risk factor. The 145 members carrying both spend $6.03M of the book's $17.75M.

But the BMI 30 threshold is an artifact, and it should be said out loud

Within smokers below BMI 30, each additional BMI point adds about $491. Above 30, about $545. The gradient barely changes — yet the step at the boundary itself is $20,195.

Real physiology does not produce a $20,000 cliff at exactly 30.0. A simulation rule does. So "move members from BMI 31 to 29" is not a defensible recommendation from this file, and any intervention priced on that threshold is priced on a generator quirk.

Age is a false lead

The intuition is that smoking gets more expensive as members age, so target older smokers. It does not hold here. The smoker-to-non-smoker gap runs $23,091 at 18–29, $23,934 at 30–39, $23,471 at 40–49 and $25,317 at 50–64 — essentially flat.

That inverts the recommendation. If the annual gap is the same at every age, the young cohort is worth more in total, because there are more years left for the saving to repeat.

The largest population is the worst target

Obese non-smokers are 561 members — 42% of the book. The entire theoretical prize from moving all of them below BMI 30 is $493k, or $879 a head. This is exactly where a general "tackle obesity" programme would spend its budget, and it is the lowest-yield option on the table.

Geography concentrates the problem

The southeast has 15.9% obese smokers against 8.9% across the other three regions, and that single segment accounts for 45.7% of all southeast spend. It is not a separate lever — it is where the cessation levers get run first.

A quarter of the variance is not in the file

A linear model on all six inputs reaches R² = 0.75. The residual is not noise: 80 members sit more than $10,000 above prediction, including 10 non-smokers billing over $30,000 with otherwise unremarkable profiles. Something not captured here — diagnosis, claims history, a catastrophic event — is driving them. That is a data collection finding, not an analytical failure.

What I would ask next

Ten questions this file cannot answer

The dataset has no time dimension: one row per member, one aggregate charge, no dates, no member ID, no claim detail. Every question below carries a metric, a window and the decision it would settle — and most of them need data this file does not contain. Listing them is the point. Knowing which question your data cannot answer is worth more than another chart of the question it can.

Where this breaks

Limitations

  • The data is simulated. It is a teaching dataset, not a real book of business. No conclusion here describes an actual insurer or population.
  • No causality is established. There is no time dimension, so nothing here can show that quitting causes a cost reduction. Every saving figure is an association-based ceiling.
  • The BMI 30 effect is almost certainly a generator artifact, and any lever that depends on it is scored low-confidence for that reason.
  • Smoking status is self-reported and binary. No intensity, no former-smoker category. Self-reported tobacco use is systematically under-declared in insurance settings.
  • Ages 18 and 19 are over-sampled — 69 and 68 records against a flat ~28 for every other age. Unweighted age trends inherit that bias.
  • Small cells are directional only. Members with four or five children (n=25 and n=18) are reported but not used to support any recommendation.
  • Charges are in an unstated price year and cannot be inflation-adjusted or benchmarked against external cost data.
